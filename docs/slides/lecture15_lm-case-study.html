We’ll focus on four variables: Sale price (response); and square footage, above-ground square footage, and number of bedrooms (potential explanatory variables).

Univariate Exploration


  • Q: How would you describe the distribution of price?
    • Right skewed, centered somewhere around $400K, most range from about $200K-$750K
  • Q: What do you expect the relationship between price and bedrooms to be like?
    • Price probably increases as # bedrooms increases

Bivariate Exploration, Multicollinearity

  • Q: Which explanatory variables would be appropriate to include in our model?
    • All three seem to have a positive linear relationship
  • Q: Do any pairs of explanatory variables suffer from multicollinearity?
    • Above ground sq. ft. and total sq. ft. We shouldn’t include both!

Model Estimation

# A tibble: 3 × 7
  term        estimate std_error statistic p_value lower_ci upper_ci
  <chr>          <dbl>     <dbl>     <dbl>   <dbl>    <dbl>    <dbl>

2 sqft_living    0.177      0.01     18.4    0        0.158    0.196
3 bedrooms     -21.6        7.27     -2.97   0.003  -35.9     -7.34
  • Q: What is the equation, including coefficient values, for the estimated model?

\[ \widehat{\text{price}} = 197 + 0.177*\text{sqrt_living} - 21.6*\text{bedrooms} \]

  • Q: How should we interpret the intercept coefficient in our model?
    • When sqrt_living and bedrooms are both 0, we expect/predict price to be $197,000 on average.
  • Q: How should we interpret the coefficient for Bedrooms? Does this match your expectation?
    • When the number of bedrooms increases by 1, we expect/predict price to decrease by $21,600 on average, holding sqft_living constant.
    • The last phrase is key! We might expect price to generally increase as the number of bedrooms increases, but the coefficient represents a very specific quantity relating to all variables in the model.

LINE Assumptions

  • Q: Does it seem like this model meets the four LINE assumptions?
    • Linearity: Possibly a small negative slope in the residual plot. Somewhat suspect.
    • Independence: Could be issues if some houses are in the same neighborhood.
    • Normality: Slight right skew in histogram, imperfect qq-plot. Not terrible but not great.
    • Equal variance: Residual plot looks fairly good and cloudlike.

In HW 4, we explored the results of the Anchoring Effect Experiment conducted during Lab 2. Our hypothesis is that the number a student saw on the paper slip (the treatment, either 82 or 531) affected the student’s guess for the number of dog breeds in the world (the response variable).

We can assess the relationship between the guessed number of dog breeds and the number received on the paper slip by running a regression. Below is a summary of this regression, where treatment is treated as a categorical explanatory variable.

  • Q2: How should we interpret the “intercept” and “treatment: 531” terms, given the context of the experiment?

Practice problem: More Linear Regression

In HW 4, we explored the results of the Anchoring Effect Experiment conducted during Lab 2. Our hypothesis is that the number a student saw on the paper slip (the treatment, either 82 or 531) affected the student’s guess for the number of dog breeds in the world (the response variable).

We can assess the relationship between the guessed number of dog breeds and the number received on the paper slip by running a regression. Below is a summary of this regression, where treatment is treated as a categorical explanatory variable.

# A tibble: 2 × 7
  term           estimate std_error statistic p_value lower_ci upper_ci

1 intercept          452.      149.      3.04   0.003    158.      746.

  • Q3: Suppose I had tested the effect of three different treatments: 82, 531, and 900, instead of just 82 and 531. What would be different about the regression model and the results table (assuming treatment is still a categorical variable)?
    • Then we would add another term to the regression model: \(+ \widehat{\beta}_2 \cdot x_{treatment:900}\). The regression summary table would show one more row for \(\widehat{\beta}_2\).
  • Q4: Suppose treatment was actually a quantitative variable, with each student seeing a different number in \([0, 200]\). Suppose that then our regression model produced estimates \(\widehat{\beta}_0 = 100\) and \(\widehat{\beta}_1 = 4\). How should we interpret \(\widehat{\beta}_0\) and \(\widehat{\beta}_1\) now?
    • \(\widehat{\beta}_0\) would then be what we expect/predict a students’ dog breed guess to be when they see the number 0.
    • \(\widehat{\beta}_1\) would be how much we expect/predict a students’ dog breed guess to increase when the number they see on the slip of paper increases by 1.
